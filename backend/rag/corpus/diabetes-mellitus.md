# Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a group of metabolic disorders characterized by
chronic hyperglycaemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion,
insulin action, or both. The two most common forms are type 1 diabetes,
an autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells leading to absolute
insulin deficiency, and type 2 diabetes, a progressive disorder defined
by insulin resistance together with relative insulin deficiency. A third
major category, gestational diabetes, develops during pregnancy and
resolves after delivery but confers a substantially elevated lifetime
risk of progressing to type 2 diabetes.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is established by any one of four confirmatory tests: a fasting
plasma glucose of 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher after an overnight
fast of at least eight hours; a 2-hour plasma glucose of 200 mg/dL
(11.1 mmol/L) or higher during a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test; a
random plasma glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher in a patient with classic
symptoms of hyperglycaemia; or a glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) of 6.5%
(48 mmol/mol) or higher. An abnormal result should be confirmed on a
second test unless the patient is clearly symptomatic. Prediabetes is
defined by an HbA1c between 5.7% and 6.4%, impaired fasting glucose of
100-125 mg/dL, or impaired glucose tolerance with a 2-hour glucose of
140-199 mg/dL, and represents a high-risk state warranting active
intervention.

## Management

Metformin is the first-line pharmacotherapy for most patients with type 2
diabetes, combined with lifestyle modification addressing diet, physical
activity, and weight. When glycaemic targets are not achieved or
metformin is contraindicated, second-line agents include sodium-glucose
cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1)
receptor agonists, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors,
sulphonylureas, and insulin. The choice of agent is guided by
cardiovascular and renal comorbidity: SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1
receptor agonists are preferred in patients with atherosclerotic
cardiovascular disease, heart failure, or chronic kidney disease. Type 1
diabetes always requires insulin therapy.

## Monitoring and targets

Glycaemic targets are individualised. For most non-pregnant adults with
type 2 diabetes a reasonable target is an HbA1c below 7.0%, with more or
less stringent targets chosen on the basis of life expectancy, burden of
comorbidity, hypoglycaemia risk, and patient preference. Self-monitoring
of blood glucose guides day-to-day management, particularly in patients
on insulin. Regular monitoring of complications is mandatory: annual
retinal examination, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio and estimated
glomerular filtration rate for nephropathy, foot examination, and
cardiovascular risk assessment.

## Complications

Chronic hyperglycaemia damages the microvasculature and the
macrovasculature. Microvascular complications comprise retinopathy,
nephropathy, and neuropathy; macrovascular complications include
coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, and peripheral arterial
disease. Diabetes is the leading cause of adult blindness, end-stage
kidney disease, and non-traumatic lower-limb amputation worldwide.
Intensive glycaemic control reduces microvascular complications, whereas
blood pressure control and statin therapy reduce macrovascular risk.
