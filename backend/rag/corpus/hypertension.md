# Hypertension and Cardiovascular Risk

Hypertension is a persistently elevated arterial blood pressure that
increases the risk of stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure,
chronic kidney disease, and death. Blood pressure is classified on the
basis of office measurements: normal is a systolic pressure below
120 mmHg and a diastolic pressure below 80 mmHg; elevated is a systolic
of 120-129 mmHg with a diastolic below 80 mmHg; stage 1 hypertension is
a systolic of 130-139 mmHg or a diastolic of 80-89 mmHg; and stage 2
hypertension is a systolic of 140 mmHg or higher or a diastolic of
90 mmHg or higher. A hypertensive crisis is a systolic pressure of
180 mmHg or higher or a diastolic of 120 mmHg or higher, often with
acute end-organ damage.

## Risk assessment

Absolute cardiovascular risk determines how aggressively hypertension is
treated. Risk factors include age, male sex, smoking, diabetes,
dyslipidaemia, obesity, physical inactivity, and a family history of
premature cardiovascular disease. A 10-year atherosclerotic
cardiovascular disease risk score above a threshold (commonly 10%)
identifies patients in whom pharmacological treatment is indicated even
at stage 1 levels. Chronic kidney disease and diabetes are themselves
potent independent risk amplifiers.

## Non-pharmacological management

Lifestyle modification is the foundation of blood pressure management:
dietary sodium restriction to below 1500-2300 mg per day, adoption of a
diet rich in fruit, vegetables, and low-fat dairy products, weight loss
in overweight patients, regular aerobic physical activity, moderation of
alcohol intake, and smoking cessation. Each intervention lowers systolic
blood pressure by 2-10 mmHg, and multiple interventions are additive.

## Pharmacological management

First-line antihypertensive classes include thiazide and thiazide-like
diuretics, calcium-channel blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)
inhibitors, and angiotensin-receptor blockers (ARBs). ACE inhibitors and
ARBs are preferred in patients with chronic kidney disease, diabetes with
albuminuria, or heart failure. In most patients blood pressure control
requires two or more agents from complementary classes, often as a fixed
combination. The commonly targeted goal is a blood pressure below
130/80 mmHg in most adults, with the understanding that targets should
be individualised to avoid adverse effects.

## Complications of untreated hypertension

Sustained hypertension damages the heart (left ventricular hypertrophy,
coronary disease, heart failure), the brain (ischaemic and haemorrhagic
stroke, cognitive decline), the kidneys (nephrosclerosis and progressive
chronic kidney disease), the retina (hypertensive retinopathy), and the
large arteries (aortic dissection, aneurysm, and peripheral arterial
disease). Adequate control reduces stroke risk by roughly 35-40%,
myocardial infarction risk by 20-25%, and heart failure risk by more
than 50%.
