# Sepsis

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated
host response to infection. Under the Sepsis-3 consensus, sepsis is
defined as a rise of two or more points in the Sequential Organ Failure
Assessment (SOFA) score in the setting of a suspected or confirmed
infection. Septic shock is a subset in which underlying circulatory and
cellular/metabolic abnormalities are profound enough to substantially
increase mortality, identified by a vasopressor requirement to maintain a
mean arterial pressure of 65 mmHg or higher combined with a serum lactate
above 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

## Recognition

Because SOFA is time-consuming at the bedside, the quick SOFA (qSOFA)
screen uses three criteria: altered mentation, a respiratory rate of
22 breaths per minute or higher, and a systolic blood pressure of
100 mmHg or lower. Two or more of these identify patients at high risk
of poor outcomes. In the emergency department and ward, screening for
sepsis is triggered by the combination of suspected infection plus
hypotension, tachycardia, tachypnoea, altered mental status, or a
raised lactate. Time to recognition is the single most important
modifiable determinant of outcome; each hour of delayed antimicrobial
therapy is associated with increased mortality.

## Initial management

Management follows a bundled approach. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign
recommends measuring lactate, obtaining blood cultures before
antibiotics where this does not delay treatment, and administering
broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics within one hour of recognition.
An initial fluid challenge of 30 mL/kg crystalloid is given in
hypotension or elevated lactate. Vasopressors, most commonly
norepinephrine, are added when hypotension persists despite fluid.
Early source control of the infection, such as drainage of an abscess or
removal of infected material, is essential. The mean arterial pressure
target is 65 mmHg. Repeat lactate measurement guides resuscitation
adequacy.

## Complications and organ dysfunction

Sepsis causes multiorgan dysfunction affecting the lungs (acute
respiratory distress syndrome), kidneys (acute kidney injury), heart
(myocardial depression), liver, coagulation system (disseminated
intravascular coagulation), and brain (sepsis-associated encephalopathy).
It is a major cause of acute kidney injury in critically ill patients,
and the presence of chronic kidney disease increases the risk and
severity of acute-on-chronic injury. Survivors frequently experience
long-term physical, cognitive, and psychological disability, termed
post-sepsis syndrome, and require structured follow-up.

## Prevention

Vaccination against influenza, pneumococcus, and other vaccine-
preventable infections, appropriate infection control, prompt treatment
of localised infections, and avoidance of indwelling catheter misuse are
the principal preventive strategies. In hospitalised patients, early
recognition and protocolised care reduce sepsis mortality.
