# Chronic Kidney Disease

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is the progressive loss of kidney structure
or function persisting for more than three months. It is defined by a
reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 60
mL/min/1.73m2, or by markers of kidney damage such as albuminuria,
urinary sediment abnormalities, electrolyte disturbances, or structural
abnormalities, whichever is present. CKD is staged from G1 (eGFR above
90) through G5 (eGFR below 15, kidney failure) using eGFR, and by
albuminuria category from A1 (normal) to A3 (severely increased).

## Causes and risk factors

The two dominant causes are diabetes mellitus and hypertension, which
together account for the majority of cases. Other causes include
glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, obstructive nephropathy,
autoimmune disease, and nephrotoxic medication exposure. Major risk
factors are diabetes, hypertension, older age, obesity,
cardiovascular disease, a family history of kidney disease, and
previous acute kidney injury. The rate of progression is determined by
the underlying cause, blood pressure control, proteinuria, and
glycaemic control.

## Laboratory markers

eGFR is estimated from serum creatinine using validated equations.
Serum creatinine alone is an insensitive marker of early disease: a
meaningful loss of function can occur while creatinine remains in the
normal range. Albuminuria, measured as a urinary albumin-to-creatinine
ratio, is both a marker of damage and a powerful predictor of
progression and cardiovascular risk. The presence of albuminuria
combined with a low eGFR identifies the highest-risk patients. Serum
potassium and phosphate tend to rise as function declines, and anaemia
of chronic disease develops from reduced erythropoietin production.

## Management

Management aims to slow progression, treat complications, and reduce
cardiovascular risk. Blood pressure control with an ACE inhibitor or
ARB is the cornerstone, particularly in the presence of albuminuria.
Glycaemic control is essential in diabetic kidney disease. Sodium
restriction, weight management, and smoking cessation are recommended.
SGLT2 inhibitors slow CKD progression independent of their glucose
effect and are recommended in diabetic and non-diabetic proteinuric
CKD. Statins reduce cardiovascular events. Patients with eGFR below 60
require medication dose adjustment and avoidance of nephrotoxins such
as NSAIDs. Later stages require management of anaemia, metabolic
acidosis, hyperphosphataemia, and secondary hyperparathyroidism, and
referral for dialysis or kidney transplantation when kidney failure
develops.

## Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury

In the critically ill, sepsis is a leading cause of acute kidney injury,
driven by hypotension, inflammation, and microcirculatory dysfunction.
Early recognition, haemodynamic support, and treatment of the
underlying infection are the principal interventions, and the presence
of CKD increases the risk of acute-on-chronic injury.
