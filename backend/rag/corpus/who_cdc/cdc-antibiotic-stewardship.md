# CDC Guidelines: Core Elements of Antibiotic Stewardship

## Overview
Antibiotic stewardship is the effort to measure and improve how antibiotics are prescribed by clinicians and used by patients. Improving antibiotic prescribing and use is critical to effectively treat infections, protect patients, and combat antibiotic resistance.

## The Core Elements of Hospital Antibiotic Stewardship Programs

### 1. Hospital Leadership Commitment
- Formal written statement supporting stewardship
- Include stewardship in quality improvement plans
- Provide staffing and financial resources
- Include in job descriptions and performance reviews

### 2. Accountability
- Appoint a single leader responsible for program outcomes
- Typically an infectious disease physician
- Ensure authority and time to execute responsibilities

### 3. Pharmacy Expertise
- Appoint a pharmacist leader (ideally with infectious disease training)
- Responsible for implementing interventions
- Monitor antibiotic use

### 4. Action - Interventions to Improve Antibiotic Use
Implement at least one policy or practice:

#### Prospective Audit and Feedback
- Review courses of high-priority antibiotics after 48-72 hours
- Provide feedback on appropriateness
- Target broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., carbapenems, vancomycin)

#### Preauthorization
- Require approval before certain antibiotics can be dispensed
- Useful for restricted agents (last-resort antibiotics)
- May create treatment delays if not managed well

#### Additional Strategies:
- **Guideline development**: Facility-specific treatment guidelines
- **Syndrome-specific interventions**: CAP, UTI, skin/soft tissue
- **Rapid diagnostic testing**: Blood culture panels, procalcitonin
- **Dose optimization**: Pharmacokinetics-based dosing
- **IV-to-oral conversion**: Switch stable patients from IV to oral
- **Time-sensitive automatic stop orders**: For surgical prophylaxis
- **Allergy assessment**: Clarify penicillin allergies (many are not true allergies)

### 5. Tracking and Reporting
Monitor antibiotic use and outcomes:
- **Days of therapy (DOT)** per 1000 patient-days
- **Defined daily doses (DDD)**
- Standardized antimicrobial administration ratio (SAAR)
- Resistance rates for key pathogens
- C. difficile infection rates
- Adverse drug events related to antibiotics

### 6. Education
- Educate prescribers about antibiotic resistance
- Share facility-specific prescribing data
- Case-based education
- Regular updates on new guidelines and evidence

## Outpatient Antibiotic Stewardship

### Key Principles:
1. At least 80% of outpatient antibiotic prescriptions should be justified
2. Use narrowest spectrum agent appropriate
3. Right dose, duration, route
4. Avoid antibiotics for viral respiratory infections
5. Prescribe shortest effective duration

### Common Conditions - Recommended Duration:
| Condition | Duration |
|-----------|----------|
| Uncomplicated cystitis | 3 days (TMP-SMX or FQ); 5 days nitrofurantoin |
| Pyelonephritis | 5-7 days (fluoroquinolone); 14 days (beta-lactam) |
| Community-acquired pneumonia | 5-7 days (if clinically stable) |
| Cellulitis | 5-7 days |
| Sinusitis | 5-7 days |
| Pharyngitis (strep) | 10 days penicillin |

## Antibiotic Timing and Sepsis

The relationship between early antibiotics and sepsis survival is clear:
- Every hour delay increases mortality by approximately 4-8%
- However, unnecessary antibiotics also cause harm (C. diff, resistance, adverse effects)
- Balance urgency of sepsis with accuracy of diagnosis
- Procalcitonin may help de-escalate but should not delay initial treatment

## Measuring Success
Key performance indicators:
1. Reduction in overall antibiotic DOT
2. Reduction in broad-spectrum antibiotic use
3. Decrease in C. difficile rates
4. Improvement in pathogen susceptibility patterns
5. No increase in sepsis mortality
